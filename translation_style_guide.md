# HALLUCINATION — English Localization Style Guide

## 1. Localization Goal

The English version of HALLUCINATION should read like a visual novel originally written in natural contemporary English while preserving the characterization, emotional tone, pacing, ambiguity, and Japanese setting of the original Japanese script.

This is localization, not word-for-word translation.

Priority order:

1. Character voice and personality
2. Emotional intent and subtext
3. Natural English
4. Scene rhythm and dramatic timing
5. Literal correspondence to Japanese wording

When literal accuracy conflicts with natural English, preserve the meaning and dramatic function rather than the Japanese sentence structure.

Do not add information that is absent from the Japanese text.

Do not explain deliberate ambiguity, foreshadowing, or implications that the original leaves unstated.


## 2. Target English Style

Use contemporary, natural American English.

Overall target:

> Natural, restrained, cinematic English suitable for a story-driven visual novel.

Avoid:

- stiff literal translation
- textbook English
- excessively literary prose
- exaggerated "anime English"
- excessive slang
- unnecessary profanity
- overly polished or verbose AI-style prose

Characters should sound like actual people speaking rather than translated Japanese characters.


## 3. Japanese Setting

The story takes place in Japan.

Preserve the Japanese cultural setting.

Do NOT unnecessarily Americanize:

- the school system
- school uniforms
- convenience stores
- karaoke
- trains
- family restaurants
- clubs and school activities
- Japanese names
- other ordinary elements of Japanese life

Translate concepts naturally when an English equivalent exists.

Examples:

コンビニ → convenience store

屋上 → rooftop

高校二年生 → second-year high school student / second-year student

Do not automatically convert Japanese school years into American terms such as "junior" or "sophomore."


## 4. Character Names

Use Given Name + Family Name when displaying full names in English.

Fixed romanization:

永山拓海 → Takumi Nagayama
菊池恵 → Megumi Kikuchi
菊池響子 → Kyoko Kikuchi
北川龍也 → Ryuya Kitagawa
佐田翔太 → Shota Sada
上田綾香 → Ayaka Ueda
高田隆二 → Ryuji Takada

Do not change romanization without explicit instruction.

Always use `Kyoko` for 菊池響子. Do not use alternative spellings such as `Kyōko` or `Kyouko`. Kyoko Kikuchi is Megumi Kikuchi's mother.

Note:
If the Japanese source contains inconsistent kanji spellings of a character name, do not invent different English names. Use the fixed English name above.


## 5. Forms of Address

Forms of address are part of characterization and relationship development.

Do NOT mechanically replace every surname with a first name.

When characters address each other by surname in Japanese because of social distance, preserve that distance naturally in English where possible.

For example, an early relationship may use:

Nagayama
Kikuchi

rather than automatically:

Takumi
Megumi

A later transition from surname-based address to first-name address may be narratively significant and must not be erased.

Japanese honorifics such as:

-san
-kun
-chan

should NOT normally be retained in English.

Preserve the social relationship through name choice, tone, and context instead.

If a specific scene depends strongly on an honorific itself, flag it for review rather than making an arbitrary decision.


## 6. Takumi Nagayama — Character Voice

Takumi is reserved, analytical, observant, and introverted.

His English should generally be:

- calm
- understated
- thoughtful
- relatively mild
- direct when necessary
- rarely flashy or slang-heavy

His dialogue should not sound overly timid unless the Japanese specifically portrays him that way.

As the story progresses, his speech can become more confident and decisive.

Avoid making Takumi sound:

- sarcastic by default
- overly eloquent
- melodramatic
- like a stereotypical shy anime protagonist

His internal narration may be more analytical than his spoken dialogue.


## 7. Megumi Kikuchi — Character Voice

Megumi is friendly, intelligent, socially comfortable, perceptive, and kind.

Her English should feel naturally conversational.

She can:

- tease lightly
- respond quickly
- soften serious conversations
- use contractions freely
- show warmth without sounding childish

Her dialogue with Takumi is especially important.

Their conversations should feel like two intelligent teenagers gradually becoming comfortable with each other.

Do not make Megumi excessively cute or give her stereotypical anime-girl speech patterns.


## 8. Ryuya Kitagawa — Character Voice

Ryuya is intimidating, aggressive, confident, and strong-willed.

He is not stupid.

His English should generally be:

- short
- direct
- confident
- impatient when appropriate

Do not turn him into a caricature of an American delinquent.

Avoid mechanically adding expressions such as:

"bro"
"dawg"
"punk"
"yo"

unless the Japanese context genuinely requires an equivalent.

Profanity should be used only when justified by the original intensity.


## 9. Shota Sada — Character Voice

Shota is rougher and more talkative than Ryuya.

He often brings a lighter or joking rhythm into conversations, but he can also be perceptive.

His English can be:

- casual
- playful
- provocative
- slightly flippant
- quicker and more expressive than Ryuya's

Do not make every line a joke.

Do not exaggerate him into comic relief.


## 10. Ayaka Ueda — Character Voice

Ayaka is blunt, dry, sharp, and somewhat cynical.

Her reactions and retorts are important to her personality.

Her English should generally be:

- concise
- natural
- dry
- slightly cutting when appropriate

She should not sound excessively formal.

Do not overuse profanity to represent her bluntness.

Her emotional moments should remain restrained when the Japanese is restrained.


## 11. Narration

Use natural contemporary English prose.

Narration should generally be:

- concise
- visual
- emotionally precise
- easy to read on a visual novel screen

Do not mechanically reproduce Japanese sentence structure.

Japanese frequently omits subjects and repeats names differently from English. Restructure sentences when necessary.

Avoid repetitive constructions such as:

"Takumi thought..."
"Takumi felt..."
"Takumi realized..."
"Takumi wondered..."

when the viewpoint already makes this obvious.

Prefer direct internal narration when natural.


## 12. Narrative Tense

Use past tense as the default narrative tense.

Maintain tense consistently unless a deliberate stylistic reason in the source requires otherwise.


## 13. Internal Monologue

Internal thoughts should sound like thoughts rather than formal narration.

If the Japanese thought is short and immediate, keep the English short and immediate.

Do not unnecessarily turn a simple thought into a complete explanatory sentence.

Preserve uncertainty.

If Takumi does not understand something yet, the English translation must not explain it for the player.


## 14. Dialogue

Translate dialogue by context, not sentence-by-sentence in isolation.

Read the lines before and after a sentence before deciding how it should sound.

Japanese conversational responses such as:

そうだね
うん
そうか
そっか
なるほど
まあ
えっと

must NOT always receive the same fixed English translation.

Depending on context, they may become:

Yeah.
Right.
I see.
Makes sense.
I guess.
Well...
Okay.
Oh.
Got it.

or may be omitted when natural English does not require a response.

Preserve the conversational function, not the dictionary definition.


## 15. Contractions

Use contractions naturally in spoken dialogue.

Prefer:

I'm
don't
can't
isn't
that's
you're
we're
I'll

when a native speaker would naturally use them.

Avoid unnaturally formal dialogue such as:

"I do not know."
"I cannot understand."

unless the character or situation specifically calls for formality.

Narration may use fewer contractions than dialogue.


## 16. Sentence Length and Rhythm

Japanese and English do not need to have identical sentence boundaries.

One Japanese sentence may become two English sentences.

Two short Japanese sentences may occasionally become one English sentence.

The goal is natural visual-novel pacing.

However, do not compress meaningful pauses merely to make the English shorter.


## 17. Silence, Pauses, and Ellipses

Silence is part of the direction.

Do not automatically fill silence with explanatory narration.

Preserve meaningful pauses.

Use:

...

for ordinary ellipses unless the project establishes another technical convention.

Use an em dash:

—

for interruptions, abrupt breaks, or strong trailing transitions when appropriate.

Do not overuse either punctuation mark.


## 18. Humor and Banter

Translate the function of humor rather than the literal wording.

If a line is:

- teasing
- sarcastic
- awkward
- playful
- dry
- intentionally stupid

the English line should produce approximately the same impression.

Do not invent jokes that change characterization or story information.


## 19. Hallucination Terminology

"HALLUCINATION" is the official English title.

幻覚 should normally be translated as:

hallucination

when referring to the central phenomenon or ability.

Depending on context, words such as:

vision
illusion
what he saw
what she saw
the experience

may be used when repeating "hallucination" would sound unnatural.

Do not mechanically describe the ability as:

"show someone a hallucination"

every time.

Choose natural phrasing while keeping the underlying mechanism consistent.


## 20. Sensitive and Intense Scenes

Preserve the tone and narrative function of intense scenes without embellishment.

Do not:

- make disturbing material more graphic than the Japanese
- add physical details
- beautify suffering
- increase sexual implications
- increase violence
- add profanity simply to make a scene feel stronger

If the Japanese is deliberately indirect or ambiguous, the English should remain indirect or ambiguous.


## 21. Profanity

Do not translate emotional intensity by automatically increasing profanity.

English profanity often sounds stronger than equivalent casual Japanese expressions.

Use profanity according to:

1. character
2. situation
3. original emotional intensity

Avoid excessive use of strong profanity.

Ryuya, Shota, and Ayaka may use rougher language than Takumi or Megumi, but their voices should not be defined solely by profanity.


## 22. TyranoScript — CRITICAL TECHNICAL RULE

Do NOT modify TyranoScript functionality while translating.

Do not translate or alter anything that defines TyranoScript structure, control, or technical behavior, including:

- tag names
- macro names
- labels
- variables
- expressions
- conditions
- jump targets
- storage paths
- filenames
- character IDs
- face IDs
- BGM, sound-effect, and image file references
- JavaScript
- scenario control flow
- technical arguments

Examples of elements that must remain technically intact include:

[p]
[r]
[bg ...]
[playbgm ...]
[playse ...]
[auto_chara ...]
[jump ...]
[if ...]
[endif]

However, translate player-visible text even when it appears as a value inside a tag. This includes:

- `text` values used by choices, `glink`, and similar tags
- chapter-display `number` values
- chapter-display `title` values
- text displayed on screen by `ptext`
- any other player-facing UI text stored in tag arguments

The rule is not "do not change tags." The rule is: **do not change tag structure or control values, but do translate player-visible strings.**

Example:

Before:

```tyranoscript
[glink text="助けに入る" target="*help"]
```

After:

```tyranoscript
[glink text="Step in" target="*help"]
```

In this example, keep the `glink` tag name, the `text=` and `target=` argument names, and the `*help` jump target unchanged. Translate only the player-visible string `助けに入る`.

If it is unclear whether a value is a player-visible string or control data, DO NOT change it. Flag it for review.


## 23. Line Breaks and TyranoScript Text Flow

Preserve `[p]` and `[r]` unless changing them is explicitly required for English layout.

Do not delete pauses or page breaks simply because English text is shorter.

Do not add large numbers of new `[p]` or `[r]` tags during translation.

If English text creates a serious display/layout problem, flag the location rather than restructuring the game automatically.


## 24. UI Text

UI translation should prioritize clarity and consistency over literal translation.

The same Japanese UI term must use the same English translation throughout the game.

Do not translate filenames or internal IDs merely because the corresponding UI text is translated.


## 25. No Unnecessary Translator Explanations

Do not insert explanations into dialogue or narration merely because an English-speaking player may be unfamiliar with a Japanese concept.

Trust context whenever possible.

Do not add translator notes to normal game dialogue.


## 26. Ambiguity and Foreshadowing

HALLUCINATION relies on suspense, incomplete information, misdirection, and later revelations.

This is critical:

NEVER clarify something simply because the translator understands what it means from later chapters.

Translate according to what the viewpoint character and player know AT THAT POINT in the story.

Preserve:

- uncertainty
- suspicious wording
- double meanings
- misleading impressions
- unanswered questions
- foreshadowing


## 27. Avoid "Translation English"

Avoid expressions that are grammatically correct but unnatural in contemporary English.

After translating dialogue, mentally ask:

> Would a native English-speaking teenager actually say this in this situation?

Then ask:

> Does this still sound specifically like this character?

If either answer is no, revise the line.


## 28. Do Not Over-Polish

Do not rewrite every sentence into elegant literary English.

Some lines are intentionally:

- plain
- awkward
- casual
- blunt
- repetitive
- emotionally restrained

Preserve these qualities when they are part of characterization or pacing.

The objective is not to make every sentence impressive.

The objective is to make the story feel natural.


## 29. Translation Decision Hierarchy

When multiple translations are possible, use this order:

Character
↓
Emotion / Subtext
↓
Natural English
↓
Scene Rhythm
↓
Literal Accuracy

Literal wording is the lowest priority when preserving it would create unnatural English.


## 30. Quality-Control Checklist

Before considering a translated scenario complete, check:

- No unintended Japanese player-visible text remains.
- TyranoScript tag structure and control values are intact.
- Player-visible text inside tag arguments has been translated.
- Labels and jump targets are unchanged.
- Filenames and storage paths are unchanged.
- Character names follow the glossary.
- Character voices remain distinct.
- Forms of address remain consistent.
- Relationship changes expressed through names are preserved.
- Hallucination terminology is consistent.
- No information has been added.
- No ambiguity has accidentally been resolved.
- No intense scene has been embellished.
- Dialogue sounds natural when read aloud.
- English text fits the intended VN pacing.


## Final Goal

An English-speaking player should ideally feel:

> "This feels like it was written in English."

while experiencing the same characters, relationships, suspense, information, and emotional progression as the Japanese version of HALLUCINATION.
